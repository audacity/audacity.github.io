The **input** is what Audacity listens to: your microphone. The **output** is
what it plays through: your headphones. Both are set in **Audio setup**, in
the middle of the top bar. By default, Audacity uses the system's default
microphone and speaker. Check both before you record.

<UIShot
  name="app/SceneAudioSetupMenu"
  alt="The Audio setup button in the top bar, with its menu open beneath it"
/>

## What you need

- **A microphone.** USB straight into the computer, or an XLR microphone
  through an audio interface.
- **Headphones.** More important than the microphone: they stop playback
  bleeding back into the recording.
- **A quiet room.** Soft furnishings, curtains and carpet reduce room
  echo. Turn off fans and close windows.

## Plug it in

How your microphone connects depends on what it is:

- **A USB microphone** goes straight into a USB port.
- **A microphone with a 3.5 mm jack** goes into the microphone input, not
  the headphone output. Many laptops have a single combined socket for a
  headset, and a microphone-only plug may need an adapter to work in it.
- **An XLR microphone** goes into an audio interface, and the interface into
  a USB port. An XLR microphone cannot connect to a computer on its own.

## Choose your devices

Pick your headphones under **Playback device**. If you hear nothing on
playback later, this is the first thing to check.

Pick your microphone under **Recording device**, by the name of the
microphone or interface.

## Set your microphone level

The microphone button in the toolbar opens the **Microphone level** flyout.
The meter follows your microphone as soon as the flyout is open: **Show mic
metering when not recording** is on by default, so nothing needs recording
to check your input.

<UIShot
  name="app/SceneRecordLevel"
  alt="The microphone button in the toolbar, with the Microphone level flyout open beneath it and the meter showing a speaking level"
/>

1. Speak at the volume you will actually use, and watch the meter.
2. Adjust the level so your normal speech peaks between **−18 dB and
   −6 dB**. Leave headroom: audio that touches 0 dB is clipped, and clipping
   cannot be repaired afterwards.

<Callout type="info" title="This changes your computer’s microphone level">
  The Microphone level slider controls the system's input level, not just
  Audacity's. Moving it also changes the microphone level in every other
  application on your computer.
</Callout>

If the meter barely moves while you speak normally, the level is too low.
Raise it here, or move closer to the microphone. A quiet recording can be
raised afterwards with the **Amplify** effect, but that raises the room
noise with it. Set the level correctly before recording.

## Hearing yourself

If you are wearing headphones, you can also switch on **Turn on input
monitoring** in the Microphone level flyout. It plays your microphone back
through your output, so you hear yourself as you speak.

<Callout type="warning" title="Headphones on before you switch monitoring on">
  Monitoring through speakers sends your microphone straight back out into the
  room, and the microphone picks that up again. The result is a feedback howl
  that gets louder the longer it runs, loud enough to damage hearing and
  equipment. Put headphones on first, set the volume low, then switch
  monitoring on.
</Callout>

<UIShot
  name="app/SceneInputMonitoring"

<Callout type="tip" title="Top tip">
  The meter can display a gradient instead of a block of colour: green is
  comfortable, amber is loud but usable, red means clipping. Choose the
  Gradient style in the playback meter's settings, opened from the meter in
  the toolbar.
</Callout>

## Mono or stereo?

One microphone produces one channel, so record a voice in **mono**.
Recording it in stereo produces two identical copies and a file twice the
size. If an interface has two inputs, give each microphone its own mono
track instead of sharing one stereo track. Use stereo for music and for
files that are already stereo.

Set the channel count under **Recording channels** in Audio setup.
