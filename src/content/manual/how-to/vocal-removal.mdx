Audacity 4 does not ship the old **Vocal Reduction and Isolation** plug-in — there is no such entry in the Effect menu. The technique behind it is still available by hand, and this page walks through it: split a stereo track into two mono tracks, invert one, and mix them back together so that everything the two channels have in common cancels out.

Read the limits first. This removes whatever sits in the centre of the mix — usually the lead vocal, but also usually the bass and the kick drum. It does nothing on a mono recording, and nothing useful where the vocal was not panned dead centre.

## Before you start

You need a stereo track and a mix in which the vocal is centred. Work on a copy, or be ready to undo — the steps below replace the original track.

## Steps

1. Select the stereo track. Open its track menu from the button beside the track name, or by right-clicking the track control panel.
2. Choose **Split stereo to center mono**. You now have two mono tracks, one holding the left channel and one the right, both panned centre so they sum on playback. (**Split stereo to L/R mono** splits the same way but leaves the two hard-panned, which suits editing channels separately, not cancelling them.) Both are described under [Track options](/manual/track-control-panel/audio-track-item#split-stereo-to-lr-mono).
3. Select one of the two new tracks and choose **Effect → Special → Invert**. Nothing sounds different yet — [Invert](/manual/effects/special/invert) flips the waveform's polarity, which only matters relative to something else.
4. Select both tracks and choose **Tracks → Mix-down to…**. The two are summed, and anything identical in both channels cancels to silence.
5. Listen. If the vocal has largely gone, the mix was centred enough. If it is still clearly there, it was not, and no adjustment will change that — undo and stop here.

## Notes

What you are left with is mono in content: the process discards exactly the information that made the recording stereo, leaving the difference between the two channels. Everything else that was centred goes with the vocal, so most mixes lose their bass and kick as well.

Reverb on the vocal is rarely centred, so it survives. On a heavily produced track that leaves an audible ghost — the tail without the voice — which is often more distracting than the vocal was.

There is no counterpart step that isolates the vocal instead. Audacity 4 has no effect that extracts the centre of a mix, and inverting the other channel gives the same cancellation. To lift one specific sound rather than the whole centre, the [Spectral tools](/manual/effects/spectral-tools) act on a chosen band of frequencies over a chosen stretch of time, which is far more surgical.

See also the reference entry for [Vocal Reduction and Isolation](/manual/effects/special/vocal-reduction-and-isolation).
