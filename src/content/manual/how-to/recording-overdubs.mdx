An overdub is a take recorded while the rest of the project plays back — a harmony over a guide vocal, a second guitar, narration over a music bed. Audacity records it onto a new track, leaving everything already in the project untouched. The thing that reliably goes wrong is timing, so the last two steps are about checking it and fixing it.

## Before you start

Use headphones. If the existing tracks are coming out of speakers, your microphone will record them along with the new part, and the two cannot cleanly be separated afterwards.

## Steps

1. Choose the input. Open **Hotbar → [Audio setup](/manual/manual-index/hotbar/audio-setup)** and set **Recording device** to the microphone or interface you are using, and **Recording channels** to 1 (Mono) or 2 (Stereo) to match it. If you plugged the device in after Audacity started, use **Rescan audio devices** first.
2. Set the recording level with the [Record level](/manual/manual-index/toolbar/metering#record-level) button in the toolbar. Play or speak the loudest part you expect and watch the meter — back the level off if it reaches the top, because clipping cannot be undone afterwards.
3. Put the playhead where the new part should begin, a bar or two before the first note rather than exactly on it.
4. Choose **Record → Record on new track**. Audacity creates an empty track, records into it, and plays everything else back as it goes. A two-channel input gets one new stereo track; otherwise you get one mono track per input channel. Choose the command again, or press <Shortcut client:load keys="space" />, to stop.
5. Listen back and check the alignment. Zoom in on a point where the new part and an existing track should hit together — a downbeat, a hand clap, the start of a word. If the new recording sits consistently late against the old, that is round-trip latency: the delay between Audacity playing the backing to you and your performance arriving back at its input.
6. Correct it in **Preferences → [Audio settings](/manual/manual-index/preferences/audio-settings)**, in the **Buffer and latency** group. **Latency compensation** is a value in milliseconds that Audacity subtracts from the start of each recording. Leave **Automatic** ticked to let Audacity work it out from the device, or untick it, measure how late a short test take lands, and enter that figure.

## Notes

The offset is constant for a given device and buffer setting, so once measured it stays measured — but check again if you change interface or buffer length. A take you have already recorded does not have to be redone: drag the clip along the timeline until it lines up.

**Buffer length** sits beside the compensation setting. Smaller buffers respond faster when overdubbing but are harder on a slow machine; larger buffers are more stable and add more delay.

To drop into an existing track partway through rather than record a whole new one, use [Lead-in recording](/manual/manual-index/header/record#lead-in-recording) (<Shortcut client:load keys="shift+d" />), which plays you the seconds before the punch point and then drops into record.
